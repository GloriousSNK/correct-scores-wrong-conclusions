\begin{abstract}
We study how different families of models forecast a chaotic mechanical system, and how
such a comparison should be scored. We construct a controlled $k$-pendulum benchmark
($k\in\{1,2,3\}$; chaotic for $k\ge2$) and evaluate four families---large language models
(LLMs), a time-series foundation model (Chronos), learned dynamics models (Neural~ODE,
Hamiltonian and Lagrangian neural networks), and classical integrators (Euler, RK4,
symplectic)---on the same out-of-sample held-out trajectories, so that learned models are
not scored on their training data. Two design choices shape the results: an out-of-sample
protocol, and a reliability-adjusted metric that assigns a fixed error to refusals and
parse failures rather than discarding them. Out of sample, the learned dynamics models
increase their error by approximately a factor of three relative to in-sample evaluation
($\sim$0.26 to 0.84--1.06\,rad) and rank below both a zero-shot time-series model and a
zero-shot LLM, and do not clear simple baselines---persistence and a nearest-neighbor analog
lookup---built from the same data. Among the black-box methods evaluated, the lowest error is obtained by an
LLM (\texttt{kimi}, $0.538$\,rad), while the known-physics integrators, which are given the
exact equations, form a ceiling by construction. The training objective affects
out-of-sample error (rollout loss is lower than derivative matching,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}1.6{\times}10^{-7}$), but mixed-$k$ rollout, which is best in-sample,
shows no significant out-of-sample effect. A matched, confound-free experiment and a direct
inference probe find little evidence that the LLMs infer hidden physical constants; under
the conditions of our benchmark they default to Earth-standard values. We report the cost of
each family alongside its error: the lowest-error black-box method is also the most expensive
per query, whereas the numerical and trained models are free at inference. In-sample
evaluation can therefore substantially overestimate learned-dynamics performance, which
indicates a need for controlled out-of-sample comparison of forecasting methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The outcome of comparing forecasting methods on a chaotic system depends on several
choices: whether models are evaluated in or out of sample, how failures are scored, and
what information each model receives. We treat these as benchmark-design questions. Current
forecasters span several families---LLMs, time-series foundation models, learned dynamics
models, and classical integrators---that are seldom evaluated on one system under identical,
out-of-sample conditions. Given the state of a dynamical system at time $t$, we ask which
family most accurately predicts the state at $t+T$, and how that comparison should be
scored. We use the $k$-link pendulum: it is integrable at $k{=}1$ and chaotic with a
positive Lyapunov exponent at $k{\ge}2$, its ground truth is computable to high precision,
and grading requires no domain expertise. All four families are evaluated on this system
under identical inputs, horizons, and metrics, with reliability and the structure of chaos
treated as part of the evaluation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A \textbf{cross-family evaluation protocol} in which all families are scored on the
same out-of-sample held-out trajectories, with a reliability-adjusted metric that assigns a
fixed error to abstentions rather than discarding them
(\S\ref{sec:metrics}, \S\ref{sec:reliability}).
\item An \textbf{out-of-sample leaderboard}: tested off their training trajectories the
learned models reach 0.84--1.06\,rad (from $\sim$0.26 in-sample) and do not clear simple
baselines (persistence, nearest-neighbor analog lookup), while among the models evaluated the
lowest black-box error is obtained by an LLM (\texttt{kimi}, 0.538\,rad). Rollout loss reduces
error relative to derivative matching, while mixed-$k$ rollout shows no significant
out-of-sample effect (\S\ref{sec:objective}).
\item \textbf{Chaos-appropriate analysis}: a predictability-horizon metric and a
confound-free system-identification test. An unadjusted regime contrast suggests lower
error when constants are hidden; a matched paired experiment and a direct inference probe
indicate that this is a difficulty confound rather than parameter inference
(\S\ref{sec:sysid}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Learned dynamics.} Neural~ODEs learn a continuous-time vector field from
data~\citep{chen2018node}. Hamiltonian Neural Networks impose an energy-conserving inductive
bias through a learned Hamiltonian~\citep{greydanus2019hnn}; Lagrangian Neural Networks
enforce the Euler--Lagrange equations through second-order autodifferentiation
\citep{cranmer2020lnn}. Chaotic systems such as the Lorenz attractor and the double pendulum
are standard testbeds~\citep{gilpin2021chaos}. We evaluate all three against general-purpose
models under matched conditions.

\paragraph{Foundation models as forecasters.} LLMs can act as zero-shot time-series
forecasters when series are serialized as text~\citep{gruver2023llmtime}, and time-series
foundation models---Chronos~\citep{ansari2024chronos}, TimesFM~\citep{das2024timesfm}, and
Moirai~\citep{woo2024moirai}---are trained for general forecasting.
\citet{zhang2025zeroshotchaos} study zero-shot forecasting of chaotic systems with such
models. We place these forecasters on the same grid as physics-informed and language models.

\paragraph{LLMs for physical and mathematical reasoning.} LLMs perform well on mathematical
problem solving~\citep{lewkowycz2022minerva} and, with chain-of-thought prompting, on
multi-step reasoning~\citep{wei2022cot}; recent benchmarks probe physics reasoning in LLMs
\citep{xu2025ugphysics}. Our task instead requires quantitative trajectory prediction of a
chaotic system rather than qualitative or symbolic reasoning.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\paragraph{Choice of system.} We use the $k$-link pendulum as a controlled system for
evaluating methodology rather than as the object of study. It provides a tunable chaos axis
(integrable at $k{=}1$, chaotic at $k{\ge}2$) that a single attractor does not; ground truth
to high precision; known governing equations, which provide an oracle ceiling and
physics-aware baselines; and controllable constants, which allow parameters to be hidden for
the system-identification test (\S\ref{sec:sysid}). A less structured system would confound
these effects, so the single controlled system is a deliberate design choice.

\subsection{Task and systems}
Given the state $\mathbf{s}_0=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k,\omega_1,\dots,\omega_k)\in
\mathbb{R}^{2k}$ of a $k$-link pendulum at $t{=}0$, the task is to predict $\mathbf{s}_T$ at
horizon $T$. Ground-truth trajectories integrate the full nonlinear Lagrangian equations of
motion with fixed-step RK4 at $\Delta t{=}10^{-3}$\,s, recording states every $0.01$\,s plus
a $10$\,s pre-$t{=}0$ context window (used only by the time-series models). Initial
conditions are sampled $\theta_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-3,3)$\,rad,
$\omega_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)$\,rad/s with a fixed seed. Because the labels are produced by
RK4 at this step, the RK4 baseline reported later (the same integrator at a $10{\times}$
coarser $\Delta t{=}10^{-2}$) is an oracle ceiling by construction rather than an independent
competitor; Euler and the symplectic integrator are distinct known-physics methods. Inputs
available to each family are stated in \S\ref{sec:inputs}.

\subsection{Regimes and grid}
Three regimes test generalization to non-standard constants (Table~\ref{tab:regimes}). In
\texttt{changed\_hidden} the constants $(g,L,m,\text{damping})$ are not disclosed to the
model, which makes the cell an in-context system-identification problem. An evaluation cell
is a tuple (model, $k$, regime, modality, horizon, prompting); horizons are
$\{0.01,1,10,60\}$\,s, modalities $\{$coords, image, image+coords$\}$ (LLMs only), and
prompting $\{$direct, chain-of-thought$\}$ (LLMs only). Non-LLM models use the
coordinate/direct setting.

\paragraph{Out-of-sample protocol.} Learned dynamics models are evaluated off their training
data. We generate a shared held-out set (a separate seed from the training trajectories) of
$180$ initial conditions ($60$ per regime, spanning $k\in\{1,2,3\}$) and evaluate every
family on the identical trajectories, keyed by \texttt{movement\_id}. The cross-family
leaderboard (\S\ref{sec:reliability}) uses horizons $\{1,10\}$\,s, where chaos has begun but
error has not saturated (\S\ref{sec:metrics}), giving $180\times2=360$ cells per model. We
report no in-sample numbers and quantify the size of the in-sample effect directly
(\S\ref{sec:reliability}).

\subsection{Inputs and grouping}
\label{sec:inputs}
Table~\ref{tab:inputs} states what each family receives. Two asymmetries favor the non-LLM
models, which makes the comparison conservative for the LLMs: (i)~Chronos is the only model
that receives the pre-$t{=}0$ history window; the LLMs and learned models receive only the
$t{=}0$ state. (ii)~The learned models receive the true constants in every regime, including
\texttt{changed\_hidden}, because they encode constants as network inputs, whereas the LLMs
receive them only when disclosed. Despite these two advantages, the learned models and
Chronos rank below an LLM that receives neither (\S\ref{sec:reliability}). A third asymmetry
runs in the opposite direction and is larger in magnitude: the LLMs are frontier-scale models
with large parameter counts and training budgets, whereas the learned models are small (a
$256$-wide, $3$-layer MLP trained on a single $8$\,GB GPU; Appendix~B). The comparison thus
contrasts off-the-shelf frontier models with small learned simulators; the behavior of a
substantially larger physics-informed network is outside the scope of this study. We group
methods into known-physics (RK4, Euler, symplectic; given the exact equations and constants)
and black-box (LLMs, Chronos, learned dynamics). The known-physics methods form a ceiling by
construction, and the comparison of interest is among the black-box methods.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
The primary error is the mean absolute angle error (radians), wrapped to $[-\pi,\pi]$ and
averaged over links and trajectories; we also track Cartesian bob error, angular-velocity
sign match, and energy drift. Three choices are specific to this setting.

\paragraph{Reliability-adjusted error.} A cell is answered only if the model returns a
parseable, finite prediction. Reporting error over answered cells alone would reward
abstention on hard cells. We therefore assign unanswered cells the random-guess baseline
$\pi/2$ (the expected wrapped-angle error of a uniform guess); we also report the strict
upper bound $\pi$. Answering is thus part of the score.

\paragraph{Predictability horizon.} For chaotic systems, pointwise error past the Lyapunov
time saturates to a system-dependent constant for every model, so long-horizon error
rankings carry little information. We define the predictability horizon as the longest
horizon a model tracks contiguously below a threshold $\tau$, starting from $t{=}0$: we walk
horizons in increasing order and stop at the first that exceeds $\tau$. The contiguous rule
is used because, after divergence, a later horizon can fall below $\tau$ by angle-wrap
coincidence. We sweep $\tau$.

\paragraph{System-identification gap.} The gap $\Delta_{\mathrm{sysid}}=
\mathrm{err}(\text{hidden})-\mathrm{err}(\text{disclosed})$ separates the cost of inferring
the constants from being given them. A model that substitutes given numbers is penalized by
hiding them ($\Delta>0$); a model that infers them in context is not ($\Delta\le0$).
Measuring this requires holding the trajectory fixed and toggling only disclosure; otherwise
disclosure is confounded with each regime's constants and initial conditions. We therefore
run a matched experiment (\S\ref{sec:sysid}): $5$ non-standard constant sets, $10$
trajectories each, $k\in\{1,2,3\}$, evaluated under disclosed and hidden conditions on
identical trajectories, with a probe that asks the hidden-condition model to report its
inferred constants.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\paragraph{LLMs.} We evaluate \texttt{kimi-k2.6}, \texttt{grok-4-1-fast-reasoning}, and
\texttt{deepseek-v4-pro} through an OpenAI-compatible API (Azure AI Foundry deployments;
evaluated June~2026), with a structured prompt (system description, disclosed constants where
applicable, initial state) and a JSON answer schema (Appendix~A). We use a uniform
configuration ($65{,}536$ max tokens, $300$\,s timeout) for all three; reasoning traces are
read from the dedicated reasoning channel when present. We take a single deterministic sample
per cell (temperature $0$); no best-of-$N$ aggregation is used, so reliability reflects the
raw per-call behavior. The API cost of the out-of-sample LLM evaluation (Parts~A and~B,
$3{,}960$ cells) was \$110.

\paragraph{Time-series foundation models.} We run Chronos~\citep{ansari2024chronos} locally
on a single GPU. Chronos is the only model given history: its adapter takes the $10$\,s
pre-$t{=}0$ window of all $2k$ state channels, resamples it onto a uniform grid of up to
$512$ points, and forecasts to the target horizon (prediction length $\le 64$ steps). The
leaderboard uses \texttt{chronos-2}, which forecasts either each channel independently
(univariate) or all $2k$ channels jointly (multivariate); this tests, with one model,
whether modeling cross-channel coupling helps on a coupled system. For that test we use $20$
trajectories per (k, regime) cell and compare the two modes paired on identical trajectories
(Wilcoxon signed-rank, bootstrap confidence intervals, Holm correction). The earlier
univariate \texttt{chronos-t5-large} was used only in a preliminary round; TimesFM is
univariate; Moirai's \texttt{uni2ts} could not be installed in our environment.

\paragraph{Learned dynamics.} Neural~ODE, HNN, and LNN share a $256$-wide, $3$-layer tanh
MLP and integrate at inference with \texttt{scipy}. We compare two objectives: derivative
matching (MSE on finite-difference $\dot{\mathbf{s}}$) and rollout loss (MSE on the state
after $k$ Euler steps, with gradients through the unroll). We also use mixed-$k$ rollout,
which trains on several window lengths jointly (Neural~ODE: $k\in\{10,50\}$; HNN:
$k\in\{5,20\}$). The LNN's loss requires second-order autodifferentiation, which Apple's MPS
backend does not run; we train it at full scale ($67$k samples, hidden 256) on a CUDA GPU.
All learned models are trained on the same nine trajectories---one per (k, regime) cell
(Appendix~B)---a deliberately small training budget whose effect we return to in the
Limitations.

\paragraph{Integrators.} Euler, RK4, and St\"ormer--Verlet (symplectic) at $\Delta
t{=}0.01$\,s using the exact equations of motion; their error is from discretization alone.
RK4 is the data-generating process and serves as an oracle ceiling rather than an independent
competitor.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We add four simple baselines as reference floors, evaluated on the
same grid; persistence and constant-velocity are trivial, while the linearized and
nearest-neighbor baselines are stronger references (nearest-neighbor is a non-parametric
analog forecast, a method used operationally in weather prediction). \emph{Persistence}
predicts $\theta_T{=}\theta_0$; \emph{constant-velocity} predicts
$\theta_T{=}\theta_0{+}\omega_0 T$; \emph{linearized} integrates the small-angle linearization
about the downward equilibrium (matrix exponential of the numerical Jacobian of the dynamics,
using the cell's true constants); and \emph{nearest-neighbor} returns the state reached $T$
later by the closest state (angle-wrapped distance) in the matching (k, regime) training
trajectory---a lookup on the same data the learned models were trained on.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Out-of-sample leaderboard}
\label{sec:reliability}
Table~\ref{tab:overall} reports the out-of-sample leaderboard on the shared held-out set
($360$ cells: $180$ trajectories $\times$ horizons $\{1,10\}$\,s), using the
reliability-adjusted metric so that an unanswered or missing cell is scored at $\pi/2$. Every
model is scored on the same grid; the Answered column gives the fraction of cells for which a
model returned a valid prediction (only the LNN, whose autograd-ODE integration diverges to
NaN on $55\%$ of cells, falls below $100\%$). LLMs are scored on their direct-prompt
forecasts. Confidence intervals are $95\%$ paired bootstrap over trajectories ($B{=}10^4$);
for the LLMs they reflect variation across trajectories only, not per-call sampling, since we
take a single temperature-$0$ sample per cell and hosted models are not perfectly
deterministic.

The classical integrators have the lowest error: RK4 (the data-generating process, hence an
oracle ceiling) at $0.059$, symplectic at $0.205$, Euler at $0.405$. Among the black-box
methods evaluated, the lowest error is obtained by \texttt{kimi} ($0.538$\,rad, $99\%$
answered), below both Chronos modes and all learned dynamics models. The four baselines
locate the floor. \texttt{kimi} is below every baseline with a non-overlapping
confidence interval (the closest, nearest-neighbor, is $0.725$), so its standing is not an
artifact of the scoring; both Chronos modes are also below the best baseline in point
estimate. The learned dynamics models are not: in point estimates the nearest-neighbor analog
lookup ($0.725$) is below \emph{every} learned model, the linearized-pendulum baseline
($0.855$) is below all but the best, and persistence ($1.004$) is below the plain Neural~ODE,
HNN, and LNN, with intervals overlapping only for the closest comparisons. Out of sample the
learned models therefore do not clear these simple baselines---in several cases not even
persistence---despite the baselines using the same training data. They score $\sim$0.26\,rad under in-sample evaluation and reach $0.84$--$1.06$\,rad
here (the lowest, mixed-$k$ rollout Neural~ODE, ranks 7th); this factor of approximately three
indicates that their in-sample strength on this benchmark is largely attributable to
evaluation on their training trajectories.

\paragraph{Effect of the reliability adjustment.} \texttt{grok} answers $82\%$ of the grid

\subsection{Behavior by horizon}
Table~\ref{tab:horizon} reports out-of-sample error by horizon. At $1$\,s the ordering
follows the leaderboard: the integrators are near-exact, \texttt{kimi} ($0.457$) has the
lowest black-box error, and the learned models are at $0.76$--$0.97$. At $10$\,s all models
are in the chaotic regime, and the ordering is stable: \texttt{kimi} ($0.619$) remains lowest
among black-box methods while the learned models reach $0.91$--$1.26$. The mixed-$k$
Neural~ODE, which is the in-sample standout at $1$--$10$\,s, is mid-pack here ($0.779$ /
$0.908$). Figure~\ref{fig:errorgrowth} shows the growth curves over the
$\{0.01,1,10,60\}$\,s ladder: the numerical integrators stay near zero until ${\sim}10$\,s,
whereas every black-box family saturates near the chaotic noise floor by $1$\,s. The
$\{1,10\}$\,s window used for the leaderboard is the range over which the families remain
separable.

\subsection{Breakdown by $k$ and by regime}
\label{sec:breakdown}
Tables~\ref{tab:perk} and~\ref{tab:perregime} (Appendix~C) report out-of-sample error by
number of links and by regime. Error increases monotonically with $k$ for every family (for
\texttt{kimi}, $0.11\to0.55\to0.95$; the integrators are near-exact at $k{=}1$ and degrade as
chaos sets in), and the cross-family ordering holds within each $k$. The
\texttt{changed\_hidden} regime has the lowest error for almost every model (for
\texttt{kimi}, $0.46$ hidden vs.\ $0.64$ normal; the learned models are lower by
$0.5$--$0.8$\,rad relative to \texttt{normal}); its low-gravity, short-rod constants produce
slower, less-divergent motion over a fixed horizon, so the unadjusted ``hidden helps''
contrast is a difficulty confound (\S\ref{sec:sysid}). Re-estimating the leaderboard
confidence intervals with a cluster bootstrap over whole held-out trajectories changes no
interval by more than ${\sim}0.03$\,rad and changes no ranking, so the gaps are not an
artifact of treating correlated cells as independent.

\subsection{Training objective}
\label{sec:objective}
Table~\ref{tab:objective} reports the effect of the loss with architecture fixed. Rollout
loss has lower error than derivative matching: for the Neural~ODE, $1.028$ drops to $0.870$
under a single-$k$ rollout loss, and in a powered paired study ($n{=}720$ trajectories,
Wilcoxon signed-rank) this difference is significant
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}1.6{\times}10^{-7}$). Penalizing trajectory error through an unrolled
integrator, rather than instantaneous derivative error, optimizes the quantity the benchmark
measures. The in-sample advantage of mixed-$k$ rollout does not hold out of sample: for the
Neural~ODE it is indistinguishable from single-$k$ ($0.844$ vs.\ $0.870$;
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.089$), and for the HNN it is higher ($1.003$ vs.\ $0.922$;
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.088$). Full-scale CUDA training of the LNN gives the highest error
($1.421$), with $55\%$ of cells diverging to NaN under autograd-ODE integration. We do not
claim that LNNs are intrinsically weak; under our implementation and protocol the LNN was
numerically unstable and generalized poorly out of sample.

\subsection{Physical fidelity: energy conservation and stability}
\label{sec:energy}
Pointwise angle error is not the axis the Hamiltonian and Lagrangian networks are designed
for; those architectures target structure preservation. We therefore evaluate energy
conservation directly on the undamped (\texttt{normal}) regime, where the true energy is
constant and HNN/LNN are well specified. Table~\ref{tab:energy} reports the mean absolute
energy drift over a $10$\,s rollout, normalized by the fixed scale $E^{*}{=}g\sum_i m_i L_i$
(0 = perfect conservation), and the fraction of rollouts that stay bounded.

Three points follow. First, the inductive bias does not translate into physical fidelity: the
symplectic integrator (a classical structure-preserving method) drifts by $0.066$, while the
lowest-drift learned model is $0.78$---worse than a plain Euler integrator ($0.494$) and an
order of magnitude above symplectic. Second, this is a property of the learned energy
function, not the rollout integrator: rolling the HNN out with a \emph{symplectic} integrator
leaves its drift essentially unchanged (e.g.\ $2.04$ vs.\ $2.07$ for hnn-rollout; $0.79$ vs.\
$0.87$ for hnn-rollout-mixed), because it conserves its \emph{learned} Hamiltonian exactly but
that Hamiltonian is a poor match to the true energy. Third, the Hamiltonian bias gives no
advantage over a plain Neural~ODE at matched objective (neural-ode-rollout-mixed $0.78 \approx$
hnn-rollout-mixed $0.87$); what reduces drift is the rollout objective, not the structure. The
LNN additionally fails on stability, with only $27\%$ of rollouts bounded. Even on the axis
they are built for, the learned models do not match the classical integrators. (As for the
leaderboard, the learned energy functions are estimated from nine trajectories;
\S\ref{sec:limitations}.)

each model tracks contiguously below a threshold $\tau$, over the ladder

\paragraph{Unadjusted regime contrast.} On the shared held-out set the three regimes differ
in both their constants and their initial conditions, so a regime contrast conflates
disclosure with difficulty. The unadjusted contrast is large: pooled across LLMs, the hidden
regime has lower error than the disclosed regime ($-0.390$\,rad,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$, unpaired) and than the normal regime ($-0.524$,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$). This is the ``LLMs do better when constants are hidden''
pattern reported in earlier rounds.

\paragraph{Matched experiment.} We ran the confound-free test of \S\ref{sec:metrics}: $5$
non-standard constant sets, $10$ trajectories each, $k\in\{1,2,3\}$, scored under disclosed
and hidden conditions on identical trajectories, paired by trajectory
(Table~\ref{tab:sysid}). The effect reverses. Hiding the constants increases error
($\Delta_{\mathrm{sysid}}{=}{+}0.069$\,rad, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.034$); at $k{=}1$
disclosure helps ($+0.297$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$) and at the $1$\,s horizon as well
($+0.145$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$), with a small reverse effect at $k{=}3$ ($-0.055$,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.016$). Per model, \texttt{kimi} is penalized by hiding
($\Delta{=}{+}0.211$), \texttt{grok} mildly ($+0.038$), and \texttt{deepseek} is flat
($-0.042$). The advantage of the hidden regime in the unadjusted contrast is therefore a
difficulty confound: the hidden-regime trajectories are easier.

\paragraph{Inference probe.} In the hidden condition we also asked each model to report its
inferred constants. The models do not recover them: against true $g\in[1.6,12]$ the
inferred-$g$ MAE is $1.81$ (\texttt{deepseek}), $1.88$ (\texttt{grok}), and $2.12$
(\texttt{kimi}). The pattern is consistent across models: they default to Earth-standard
values (\texttt{deepseek} reports $g{\approx}9.81$ on nearly every cell; \texttt{grok} and
\texttt{kimi} report $L{=}m{=}1.0$ on nearly every cell, a constant output for which the
rank correlation with truth is undefined). The one nonzero signal is \texttt{deepseek}'s rod
lengths, which correlate with truth at Spearman $0.11$ ($p{=}0.007$). We therefore find
little evidence that the accuracy difference between regimes is caused by in-context system
identification.

\subsection{Univariate versus multivariate time-series modeling}
\label{sec:multivariate}
The leaderboard uses one trajectory per cell, which resolves the large gaps between families
but not small effects. To test whether a time-series model that shares information across the
$2k$ state channels benefits from their coupling, we compare Chronos-2 in univariate and
multivariate modes with $20$ trajectories per (k, regime) cell ($n{=}720$ paired samples) on
identical trajectories. The two modes are not distinguishable: mean error $0.549$ (uni, $95\%$
CI $[0.505,0.593]$) vs.\ $0.543$ (multi, $[0.498,0.589]$). The paired difference is
$+0.005$\,rad overall, with no significant effect at any $k$ or at horizons $1$\,s and
$10$\,s; the per-$k$ trend at $n{=}1$ (multivariate appearing to help most at $k{=}3$) goes
to zero at $n{=}720$. A nominal overall $p{=}0.025$ is driven by a sub-$0.01$\,rad bias at
the $0.01$\,s horizon and does not survive Holm correction. We report a null result:
cross-channel coupling gives no measurable benefit for time-series forecasting of this system.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{In-sample evaluation.} Scored on held-out rather than training trajectories, the
learned dynamics models triple their error and fall below a zero-shot time-series model, a
zero-shot LLM, and simple baselines. The ``small fitted model beats general-purpose
forecasters'' conclusion an in-sample leaderboard invites does not survive; a learned
simulator must be reported on trajectories it never saw. What transfers among the learned
models is the rollout objective, not the architecture or the mixed-$k$ recipe.

\paragraph{Reliability.} Scoring abstentions at $\pi/2$, rather than averaging over answered
cells, is what places \texttt{grok} and the LNN low; chain-of-thought does not help (paired
CoT-vs-direct differences are insignificant). A fixed budget of reasoning tokens does not
substitute for sequential integration.

\paragraph{System identification.} The apparent ``hidden helps'' effect is a difficulty
confound: on matched trajectories, hiding the constants slightly increases error, and the
probe shows the models default to Earth-standard values rather than inferring the true ones.
We therefore find little evidence of in-context system identification, and caution against
reading regime contrasts as inference without a matched control.

\paragraph{Cost and scope.} The LLM evaluation is the only family billed per query (\$110,
${\sim}\$0.03$/forecast); the others are free at inference, so the lowest-error black-box
method is also the most expensive. The quantitative ranking is specific to this system, but
the protocol is not---in-sample optimism, reliability-adjusted scoring, and the matched
disclosure control are system-agnostic, and the harness is substrate-parametrized---so
applying it to other chaotic systems is a direct extension, one we expect to change the
ranking but not the methodology.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
All results use a single held-out seed; additional seeds would tighten the error bars.
Paired tests are Holm-corrected within each
family (objective, multivariate, system-ID) but not across the paper, so marginal
results---for example the overall system-identification gap
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.034$)---should be read as suggestive. Predictions are single-endpoint;
a full-rollout protocol would let the predictability horizon be measured directly. The metric
is pointwise; we report energy conservation (\S\ref{sec:energy}) but leave fuller probabilistic
(CRPS/ensemble) and attractor-distribution scoring, and a noisy-initial-condition version, to
future work. The comparison is structurally asymmetric: the learned models are trained on the
$k$-pendulum family and tested on held-out trajectories of it, whereas the LLMs and Chronos
are zero-shot. The learned models are trained
on only nine trajectories (one per (k, regime) cell), a small budget for a chaotic system; the
finding that they do not exceed simple baselines is specific to this training budget, and a
data- or capacity-scaling study is needed to separate architecture from data limitations. The
Hamiltonian and Lagrangian architectures also conserve energy by construction and cannot
represent dissipation, so the two damped regimes disadvantage them in the pooled leaderboard
independently of quality; the conservation test (\S\ref{sec:energy}) is therefore run only on
the undamped regime. The
pendulum equations are common in training corpora, so contamination cannot be excluded; the
hidden-constants regime mitigates this only in part. Finally, the numerical results are demonstrated on a single
system and three LLMs; extending them to other chaotic systems (Lorenz, driven pendulum) and
a broader model set (e.g.\ Qwen-VL, TimesFM, Moirai) through the same harness is left to
future work.

\section{Conclusion}
On a controlled $k$-pendulum benchmark evaluated on shared out-of-sample trajectories,
in-sample evaluation of learned dynamics is optimistic: off their training data the learned
models triple their error and do not exceed simple baselines, so the lowest black-box error
is an LLM (\texttt{kimi}) while the numerical integrators remain the ceiling. The training
objective, not the architecture, transfers; reliability-adjusted scoring and a matched,
confound-free control overturn the naive reading and find little evidence of in-context
system identification. The durable contribution is the protocol, not the version-specific
ranking.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Code, prompts (Appendix~A), trained weights, the shared held-out
specification, and per-cell checkpoints are released with the benchmark. Training
trajectories use seed $42$; the held-out evaluation set uses a disjoint seed
($\texttt{20260620}$), $180$ trajectories ($60$ per regime). LLMs (\texttt{kimi-k2.6},
\texttt{grok-4-1-fast-reasoning}, \texttt{deepseek-v4-pro}, via Azure AI Foundry, evaluated
June~2026) are sampled once at temperature $0$; hosted deployments may change over time and
temperature-$0$ API sampling is not guaranteed to be reproducible, so the exact LLM numbers
may not be reproducible from the API alone, although the protocol and all local (non-LLM)
results are. A cell is answered if it returns a parseable, finite prediction; otherwise it is
assigned $\pi/2$. All local training and evaluation run on a single consumer GPU (8\,GB); no
proprietary training data is used.

\section{Training and compute}
All learned models use a $256$-wide, $3$-layer tanh MLP, Adam, and a cosine-annealed learning
rate, on a single NVIDIA GTX~1070~Ti (8\,GB). Mixed-$k$ rollout adds negligible parameters;
its cost is wall-clock (the HNN mixed-$k$ run is the most expensive at $\sim$6.7\,h because
each unroll step recomputes $\nabla H$ via autograd). Chronos runs at inference only. The
dataset has $67{,}491$ samples across $k\in\{1,2,3\}$ and three regimes, subsampled at
$\Delta t{=}0.01$\,s from the $10^{-3}$\,s ground truth. These samples are drawn from
\emph{nine} trajectories---one per (k, regime) cell---so, while the sample count is large, the
number of distinct trajectories the learned models see is small.
