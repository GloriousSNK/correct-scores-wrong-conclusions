\begin{abstract}
Can a general-purpose model forecast a chaotic physical system out of distribution,
and how should such a comparison even be scored? We build a controlled $k$-pendulum
benchmark ($k\in\{1,2,3\}$; chaotic for $k\ge2$) that evaluates four model
families---large language models (LLMs), a time-series foundation model (Chronos),
learned dynamics models (Neural~ODE, Hamiltonian and Lagrangian neural networks), and
classical integrators (Euler, RK4, symplectic)---on the \emph{same held-out
trajectories}, so the learned models are never scored on their own training data. Two
methodological choices drive the findings: an \emph{out-of-sample} protocol, and a
\emph{reliability-adjusted} metric that scores a model's refusals and parse failures
rather than discarding them. The results invert the in-sample story. Tested off their
training trajectories the learned dynamics models roughly triple their error
($\sim$0.26 $\to$ 0.84--1.06\,rad) and fall behind both a zero-shot time-series model
and a frontier LLM; among \emph{black-box} methods the best forecaster is an LLM
(\texttt{kimi}, $0.538$\,rad), while the known-physics integrators---given the exact
equations---remain the ceiling by construction. The training \emph{objective}
generalises (rollout loss beats derivative matching,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}1.6{\times}10^{-7}$), but the in-sample ``mixed-$k$ wins'' result
does not (a statistical null out of sample). Finally, a confound-free matched
experiment plus a direct inference probe find \emph{little evidence} that LLMs infer
hidden physical constants---under our benchmark's information conditions they default
to Earth-standard priors. The takeaway is methodological: in-sample evaluation can
substantially overestimate learned-dynamics performance, underscoring the importance of
controlled out-of-sample benchmarks when comparing scientific forecasting methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Comparing forecasting methods for chaotic physical systems is deceptively hard: the
verdict depends on whether models are tested in or out of sample, on how failures are
scored, and on whether different families are given comparable information. We treat this
as a \emph{benchmark-methodology} problem. Modern forecasters span very different
families---large language models (LLMs), time-series foundation models, learned dynamics
models, and classical integrators---yet are rarely placed on one substrate under
identical, out-of-sample conditions. Given the state of a dynamical system at time $t$,
which family best predicts the state at $t+T$, and how should that comparison be scored?
The $k$-link pendulum is an ideal probe. Single
pendulums ($k{=}1$) are integrable; double and triple pendulums ($k{\ge}2$) are
chaotic with a positive Lyapunov exponent. The task is unambiguous (predict
angles and angular velocities), ground truth is computable to arbitrary
precision, and grading requires no domain expertise.

Prior evaluations tend to study one family in isolation: LLMs on physics word
problems, time-series foundation models on forecasting suites, or
physics-informed networks on chaotic benchmarks. We instead place all four
families on a single mechanical substrate under \emph{identical} inputs, horizons,
and metrics, and we take reliability and the structure of chaos seriously as
first-class evaluation concerns rather than footnotes.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A \textbf{reusable, cross-family evaluation protocol} (LLMs, a time-series
foundation model, learned dynamics, and integrators) in which all families are scored on
the \emph{same out-of-sample held-out trajectories} and a \textbf{reliability-adjusted
metric} scores abstentions instead of discarding them---instantiated on the $k$-pendulum
as a controlled testbed (\S\ref{sec:metrics}, \S\ref{sec:reliability}).
\item An \textbf{out-of-sample leaderboard that inverts the in-sample story}: tested
off their training trajectories the learned models degrade substantially ($\sim$0.26 $\to$
0.84--1.06\,rad), and among the models evaluated the strongest black-box forecaster is a
frontier LLM (kimi,
0.538\,rad). The training objective still matters---rollout loss beats derivative
matching significantly---but mixed-$k$ rollout, the in-sample winner, is a
statistical null out-of-sample (\S\ref{sec:objective}).
\item \textbf{Chaos-appropriate analysis}: a predictability-horizon metric (only
numerical integrators track past $1$\,s at strict thresholds) and a
\emph{confound-free} system-identification test. A naive regime contrast suggests
LLMs do better with constants hidden; a matched-constant paired experiment plus a
direct inference probe shows this is a difficulty confound---the LLMs do not infer
the constants but fall back on Earth-standard priors (\S\ref{sec:sysid}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Learned dynamics.} Neural~ODEs learn a continuous-time vector field
from data~\citep{chen2018node}. Hamiltonian Neural Networks impose an
energy-conserving inductive bias by deriving dynamics from a learned
Hamiltonian~\citep{greydanus2019hnn}; Lagrangian Neural Networks enforce the
Euler--Lagrange equations through second-order autodifferentiation
\citep{cranmer2020lnn}. Chaotic systems such as the Lorenz attractor and the
double pendulum are standard testbeds for such models
\citep{gilpin2021chaos}. We benchmark all three against general-purpose models
under matched conditions.

\paragraph{Foundation models as forecasters.} LLMs can act as zero-shot
time-series forecasters when series are serialised as text~\citep{gruver2023llmtime},
and dedicated time-series foundation models---Chronos~\citep{ansari2024chronos},
TimesFM~\citep{das2024timesfm}, and Moirai~\citep{woo2024moirai}---are trained for
general forecasting. \citet{zhang2025zeroshotchaos} study zero-shot forecasting of
chaotic systems with such models. Our benchmark adds these forecasters to the same
grid as physics-informed and language models.

\paragraph{LLMs for physical and mathematical reasoning.} LLMs show strong results
on mathematical problem solving~\citep{lewkowycz2022minerva} and, with
chain-of-thought prompting, on multi-step reasoning~\citep{wei2022cot}. Recent
benchmarks probe physics reasoning in LLMs more directly
\citep{xu2025ugphysics}.
Our task differs: it demands quantitative trajectory prediction of a chaotic
system, not qualitative or symbolic reasoning.

\section{Benchmark Design}

\paragraph{Why the $k$-pendulum?} We treat the $k$-link pendulum not as the object of
study but as a \emph{controlled instrument} for measuring evaluation methodology. Four
properties make it uniquely suited. It offers a \emph{tunable} chaos axis---integrable at
$k{=}1$, chaotic with a positive Lyapunov exponent at $k{\ge}2$---that a single attractor
cannot provide; its ground truth is computable to arbitrary precision; its governing
equations are \emph{known}, supplying both an oracle ceiling and physics-aware baselines;
and its physical constants are freely \emph{controllable}, which lets us hide parameters
and run the confound-free system-identification test of \S\ref{sec:sysid}. A less
structured chaotic system would confound the very effects we set out to isolate, so the
single, maximally-controlled substrate is a deliberate design choice rather than a
limitation of scope.

\subsection{Task and systems}
Given the state $\mathbf{s}_0=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k,\omega_1,\dots,\omega_k)\in
\mathbb{R}^{2k}$ of a $k$-link pendulum at $t{=}0$, predict $\mathbf{s}_T$ at a
horizon $T$. Ground-truth trajectories integrate the full nonlinear Lagrangian
equations of motion with \textbf{fixed-step RK4 at $\Delta t{=}10^{-3}$\,s}, recording
states every $0.01$\,s plus a $10$\,s pre-$t{=}0$ context window (used only by the
time-series models). Initial conditions are sampled $\theta_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-3,3)$\,rad,
$\omega_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)$\,rad/s with a fixed seed. Because the labels are
themselves produced by RK4 (at this fine step), the RK4 \emph{baseline} reported later
(the same integrator at a $10{\times}$ coarser $\Delta t{=}10^{-2}$) is an \emph{oracle
ceiling by construction} rather than an independent competitor; Euler and the symplectic
integrator are genuinely distinct known-physics methods. We make the inputs available to
each family explicit in \S\ref{sec:inputs}.

\subsection{Regimes and grid}
Three regimes test generalisation to non-standard constants
(Table~\ref{tab:regimes}). In \texttt{changed\_hidden} the constants $(g,L,m,
\text{damping})$ are \emph{not} disclosed to the model, turning the cell into an
in-context system-identification problem. An evaluation \emph{cell} is a tuple
(model, $k$, regime, modality, horizon, prompting); horizons are
$\{0.01,1,10,60\}$\,s, modalities $\{$coords, image, image+coords$\}$ (LLMs only),
and prompting $\{$direct, chain-of-thought$\}$ (LLMs only). Non-LLM models collapse
to the coordinate / direct setting.

\paragraph{Out-of-sample protocol.} The central methodological point of this paper
is that learned dynamics models must be tested off their training data. We
therefore generate a \emph{shared held-out set} (a separate seed from the training
trajectories) of $180$ initial conditions ($60$ per regime, spanning
$k\in\{1,2,3\}$) and evaluate \emph{every} family on the identical trajectories,
keyed by \texttt{movement\_id}. The cross-family leaderboard
(\S\ref{sec:reliability}) is computed on the discriminating horizons
$\{1,10\}$\,s, where chaos has begun to bite but error has not yet saturated
(\S\ref{sec:metrics}), giving $180\times2=360$ comparison cells per model. Earlier
in-sample numbers---where the learned models were scored on their own training
trajectories---are reported nowhere in this version; we quantify the size of that
leak directly (\S\ref{sec:reliability}).

\subsection{What each model is given, and a fair grouping}
\label{sec:inputs}
Table~\ref{tab:inputs} states exactly what each family receives. Two asymmetries are
worth naming up front, and both \emph{favour the non-LLM models}, which makes our
headline LLM result conservative: (i)~the time-series model (Chronos) is the only one
that receives the pre-$t{=}0$ \emph{history} window; the LLMs and learned models see
only the single $t{=}0$ state. (ii)~The learned models are handed the \emph{true}
constants in every regime---including \texttt{changed\_hidden}, since they encode
constants as network inputs---whereas the LLMs are told the constants only when
disclosed. Despite both advantages, the learned models and Chronos are beaten
out-of-sample by an LLM that gets neither history nor hidden constants
(\S\ref{sec:reliability}). A countervailing asymmetry, however, runs in the
\emph{opposite} direction and is larger in magnitude: the LLMs are frontier-scale models
with enormous parameter counts and training budgets, whereas the learned dynamics models
are deliberately small (a $256$-wide, $3$-layer MLP trained on a single $8$\,GB GPU;
Appendix~B). Our comparison therefore contrasts off-the-shelf frontier models with small,
\emph{accessible} learned simulators; how a substantially larger physics-informed network
would fare is an open question we do not settle here.

We accordingly group methods into \textbf{known-physics} (RK4, Euler, symplectic ---
given the exact equations and constants) and \textbf{black-box / data-driven} (LLMs,
Chronos, learned dynamics). The known-physics methods are a ceiling by construction;
the scientific question is which \emph{black-box} family forecasts best.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
The primary error is the mean absolute angle error (radians), wrapped to
$[-\pi,\pi]$ and averaged over links and trajectories; we also track Cartesian bob
error, angular-velocity sign match, and energy drift. Three evaluation choices are
specific to this setting.

\paragraph{Reliability-adjusted error.} A cell is \emph{answered} only if the model
returns a parseable, finite prediction. Reporting error over answered cells alone
rewards a model for abstaining on hard cells. We therefore report a
reliability-adjusted error in which unanswered cells are scored at the random-guess
baseline $\pi/2$ (the expected wrapped-angle error of a uniformly random guess);
we also report the strict upper bound $\pi$. This makes \emph{answering} part of
the score, not a separate column to be overlooked.

\paragraph{Predictability horizon.} For chaotic systems, pointwise error past the
Lyapunov time saturates to a system-dependent constant for \emph{every} model, so
long-horizon error rankings are weakly informative. We define the predictability
horizon as the longest horizon a model tracks \emph{contiguously} below a threshold
$\tau$, starting from $t{=}0$: we walk horizons in increasing order and stop at the
first that exceeds $\tau$. The contiguous rule is deliberate---once a trajectory has
diverged, a later horizon can dip back below $\tau$ by angle-wrap coincidence, which
is not predictability. We sweep $\tau$ to expose threshold sensitivity.

\paragraph{System-identification gap.} The gap $\Delta_{\mathrm{sysid}}=
\mathrm{err}(\text{hidden})-\mathrm{err}(\text{disclosed})$ isolates the cost of
\emph{inferring} the constants versus being told them. A model that merely
substitutes given numbers is hurt by hiding them ($\Delta>0$); a model that infers
them in context is not ($\Delta\le0$). Measuring this cleanly requires holding the
trajectory fixed and toggling \emph{only} disclosure---otherwise disclosure is
confounded with the (different) constants and initial conditions of each regime. We
therefore run a dedicated matched experiment (\S\ref{sec:sysid}): $5$ non-standard
constant sets, $10$ trajectories each, $k\in\{1,2,3\}$, evaluated under disclosed
and hidden conditions \emph{on identical trajectories}, with a direct probe that
asks the hidden-condition model to report its inferred constants.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{LLMs.} We evaluate \texttt{kimi-k2.6}, \texttt{grok-4-1-fast-reasoning},
and \texttt{deepseek-v4-pro} through an OpenAI-compatible API (Azure AI Foundry
deployments; evaluated June~2026), with a structured prompt (system description,
disclosed constants where applicable, initial state) and a JSON answer schema
(verbatim in Appendix~A). Reasoning models that emit long
chains-of-thought require large token budgets and timeouts, so we use a uniform
reliability-fixed configuration ($65{,}536$ max tokens, $300$\,s timeout) for all
three; reasoning traces are parsed from the dedicated reasoning channel when
present. We take a \emph{single} deterministic sample per cell (temperature $0$); no
best-of-$N$ aggregation is used, so reliability reflects the raw per-call behaviour.
The total API cost of the out-of-sample LLM evaluation (Parts~A and~B,
$3{,}960$ cells) was \$110.

\paragraph{Time-series foundation models.} We run Chronos~\citep{ansari2024chronos}
locally on a single GPU. Chronos is the \emph{only} model given history: its adapter
takes the $10$\,s pre-$t{=}0$ window of all $2k$ state channels (the
\texttt{pre\_context} window), resamples it onto a uniform grid of up to $512$ points,
and autoregressively forecasts to the target horizon (prediction length capped at the
model's supported range, $\le 64$ steps). The out-of-sample
leaderboard uses \texttt{chronos-2}, which forecasts either each channel
independently (\emph{univariate}) or all $2k$ channels jointly (\emph{multivariate});
this lets us test, with one model, whether modelling cross-channel coupling helps on a
coupled dynamical system. For that test we run a dedicated study with $20$
trajectories per (k, regime) cell and compare the two modes \emph{paired} on
identical trajectories (Wilcoxon signed-rank with bootstrap confidence intervals and
Holm correction). (The earlier univariate \texttt{chronos-t5-large} served only in a
preliminary round; TimesFM is univariate and adds no multivariate signal; Moirai's
\texttt{uni2ts} could not be installed in our environment---all are left to future
rounds.)

\paragraph{Learned dynamics.} Neural~ODE, HNN, and LNN share a $256$-wide,
$3$-layer tanh MLP and integrate at inference with \texttt{scipy}. We compare two
objectives: \emph{derivative matching} (MSE on finite-difference $\dot{\mathbf{s}}$)
and \emph{rollout loss} (MSE on the state after $k$ Euler steps, with gradients
flowing through the unroll). We introduce \emph{mixed-$k$ rollout}, which trains on
several window lengths jointly (Neural~ODE: $k\in\{10,50\}$; HNN: $k\in\{5,20\}$),
optimising short, medium, and long horizons at once. The LNN's loss needs
second-order autodifferentiation, which Apple's MPS backend cannot run; we train it
at full scale ($67$k samples, hidden 256) on a CUDA GPU---the configuration its
prior incarnation could not reach.

\paragraph{Integrators.} Euler, RK4, and St\"ormer--Verlet (symplectic) at
$\Delta t{=}0.01$\,s using the exact equations of motion; their error is purely from
discretisation. RK4 is the data-generating process and therefore serves as an oracle
ceiling rather than an independent competitor.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Out-of-sample leaderboard}
\label{sec:reliability}
Table~\ref{tab:overall} ranks all $14$ models plus an oracle ceiling on the shared out-of-sample
held-out set ($360$ cells: $180$ trajectories $\times$ horizons $\{1,10\}$\,s),
using the reliability-adjusted metric so that an unanswered or missing cell is
scored at the random-guess baseline $\pi/2$ rather than dropped. Every model is
scored on the \emph{identical} grid; \texttt{n\_missing} counts cells a model never
produced (only the LNN, whose autograd-ODE integration diverges to NaN on $55\%$ of
cells, is affected). LLMs are scored on their direct-prompt forecasts, the setting
all families share. Confidence intervals are $95\%$ paired bootstrap ($B{=}10^4$).

Three things stand out. First, classical integrators dominate: RK4 (the
data-generating process, hence an oracle ceiling) at $0.059$, symplectic at $0.205$,
Euler at $0.405$. Second, \textbf{among the models evaluated, \texttt{kimi} is the
strongest black-box forecaster}---at $0.538$\,rad ($99\%$ answered)---ahead of both
Chronos modes and, strikingly, \emph{every} learned dynamics model. Third, the learned
models, which in an in-sample evaluation scored ${\sim}0.26$\,rad, degrade substantially
to $0.84$--$1.06$\,rad once
they are tested on trajectories they were not trained on: the best of them
(mixed-$k$ rollout Neural~ODE, $0.844$) ranks $6$th, behind the integrators, a
zero-shot LLM (\texttt{kimi}), and both zero-shot Chronos modes. This $\sim$3$\times$ gap between in-sample and
out-of-sample error is the single most important number in the paper: it means the
widely-reported strength of small learned dynamics models on this benchmark was
substantially an artefact of evaluating them on their own training data.

\paragraph{Reliability is load-bearing.} The success column is not a footnote.
\texttt{grok} answers only $82\%$ of the held-out grid and \texttt{lnn} only $45\%$;
scoring those abstentions at $\pi/2$ (rather than averaging over answered cells
alone, which would reward them for skipping hard cells) is what places them at ranks
$8$ and $14$. A naive answered-only leaderboard would flatter exactly the least
reliable models. We regard answering as part of the score, not a separate column to
be overlooked.

\subsection{Behaviour by horizon}
Table~\ref{tab:horizon} splits the out-of-sample error by horizon. At $1$\,s the
ordering already mirrors the leaderboard: the integrators are near-exact, \texttt{kimi}
($0.457$) is the strongest black-box model, and the learned models sit at
$0.76$--$0.97$. At $10$\,s every model is well into the chaotic regime, but the
ranking is stable rather than scrambled---\texttt{kimi} ($0.619$) still leads the
non-numerical field while the learned models drift to $0.91$--$1.26$. Notably the
mixed-$k$ Neural~ODE, which the in-sample evaluation singled out as the standout at
$1$--$10$\,s, is here merely mid-pack ($0.779$ / $0.908$), worse than a zero-shot
LLM at both horizons. The qualitative LLM signature from earlier rounds---confident
near $t{=}0$, regressing to a generic chaotic guess later---is consistent with these
numbers but is no longer a disqualifier relative to the (now equally struggling)
learned models. Figure~\ref{fig:errorgrowth} plots the full growth curves over the
$\{0.01,1,10,60\}$\,s ladder and makes the divergence structure explicit: the numerical
integrators stay near zero until ${\sim}10$\,s, whereas every \emph{black-box} family
saturates near the chaotic noise floor almost immediately and is essentially flat from
$1$\,s onward---so the $\{1,10\}$\,s window used for the leaderboard is exactly where the
families are still separable.

\subsection{Breakdown by $k$ and by regime}
\label{sec:breakdown}
Tables~\ref{tab:perk} and~\ref{tab:perregime} (Appendix~C) split the out-of-sample error
by number of links and by regime. Two patterns matter. First, \textbf{difficulty scales
with $k$ for every family}: error rises monotonically from $k{=}1$ to $k{=}3$ (e.g.\
\texttt{kimi} $0.11\to0.55\to0.95$; the integrators are near-exact at the integrable
$k{=}1$ and degrade only with the onset of chaos), and the cross-family ordering is
preserved within each $k$---so the leaderboard spread reflects the dynamics, not a single
hard slice. Second, and crucially for \S\ref{sec:sysid}, \textbf{the
\texttt{changed\_hidden} regime is the \emph{easiest}, not the hardest}, for almost every
model (\texttt{kimi} $0.46$ hidden vs.\ $0.64$ normal; the learned models fall by
$0.5$--$0.8$\,rad relative to \texttt{normal}). Its low-gravity, short-rod constants
simply produce slower, less-divergent motion over a fixed horizon---so the unmatched
``hidden helps'' contrast is a difficulty confound, exactly as the matched experiment of
\S\ref{sec:sysid} confirms. Finally, re-estimating the leaderboard CIs with a
\textbf{cluster bootstrap that resamples whole held-out trajectories} rather than
individual cells widens no interval by more than ${\sim}0.03$\,rad and changes no ranking;
the headline gaps are not an artefact of treating correlated cells as independent.

\subsection{The training objective matters; mixed-$k$ does not}
\label{sec:objective}
Table~\ref{tab:objective} isolates the effect of the loss while holding the
architecture fixed, on the out-of-sample grid. One effect is robust: \textbf{rollout
loss decisively beats derivative matching.} For the Neural~ODE, derivative matching
($1.028$) drops to $0.870$ under a single-$k$ rollout loss; in a dedicated powered
paired study ($n{=}720$ trajectories, Wilcoxon signed-rank) this gap is highly
significant ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}1.6{\times}10^{-7}$). Penalising trajectory error
through an unrolled integrator, rather than instantaneous derivative error, directly
optimises what the benchmark measures.

The in-sample story does \emph{not} survive contact with out-of-sample data,
however. \textbf{Mixed-$k$ rollout---training on several window lengths jointly,
the previously reported headline winner---is a statistical null.} For the Neural~ODE
it is indistinguishable from single-$k$ ($0.844$ vs.\ $0.870$; powered
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.089$), and for the HNN it is numerically \emph{worse}
($1.003$ vs.\ $0.922$; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.088$). What looked like an
architecture-agnostic benefit of mixing horizons was, on held-out trajectories,
within noise. Finally, full-scale CUDA training of the LNN---finally running the
second-order autograd its loss requires---leaves it the \emph{worst} model
($1.421$), with $55\%$ of cells diverging to NaN under our autograd-ODE integration.
We do not claim LNNs are intrinsically weak; rather, \emph{under our implementation and
evaluation protocol} the LNN was numerically unstable and generalised poorly out of
sample.

\paragraph{A confounded ``hidden helps'' effect\dots} On the shared held-out set the
three regimes differ in both their constants \emph{and} their initial conditions, so
a regime contrast conflates disclosure with difficulty. Taken at face value that
contrast is striking: pooled across LLMs, the hidden regime has \emph{lower} error
than the disclosed regime ($-0.390$\,rad, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$, unpaired)
and than the normal regime ($-0.524$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$). Read naively,
this is the ``LLMs do better when you hide the constants'' result reported in earlier
rounds, and it is tempting to call it in-context system identification.

\paragraph{\dots that a matched experiment dismantles.} We therefore ran the
confound-free test of \S\ref{sec:metrics}: $5$ non-standard constant sets, $10$
trajectories each, $k\in\{1,2,3\}$, scored under disclosed and hidden conditions on
\emph{identical} trajectories, paired by trajectory (Table~\ref{tab:sysid}). The
effect reverses. Overall, hiding the constants makes predictions slightly
\emph{worse}, not better ($\Delta_{\mathrm{sysid}}{=}{+}0.069$\,rad,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.034$); at $k{=}1$ disclosure helps strongly
($+0.297$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$) and at the $1$\,s horizon as well
($+0.145$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$), with only a small reverse effect at
$k{=}3$ ($-0.055$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.016$). Per model, \texttt{kimi} is clearly
hurt by hiding ($\Delta{=}{+}0.211$), \texttt{grok} mildly ($+0.038$), and
\texttt{deepseek} is flat ($-0.042$). The apparent advantage of the hidden regime in
the unmatched contrast was thus a difficulty confound---the hidden-regime
trajectories were simply easier---not evidence of inference.

\paragraph{The inference probe confirms it.} In the hidden condition we additionally
asked each model to report its inferred constants. The models do \emph{not} recover
them: against true $g\in[1.6,12]$ the inferred-$g$ MAE is $1.81$ (\texttt{deepseek}),
$1.88$ (\texttt{grok}) and $2.12$ (\texttt{kimi}). The failure mode is uniform---they
fall back on Earth-standard priors: \texttt{deepseek} answers $g{\approx}9.81$ on
essentially every cell, and \texttt{grok} and \texttt{kimi} answer $L{=}m{=}1.0$ on
essentially every cell (a constant output, so the inferred-vs-true rank correlation
is undefined). The one weak signal is \texttt{deepseek}'s rod lengths, which
correlate with truth at Spearman $0.11$ ($p{=}0.007$). There is, in short, little
evidence that any accuracy difference between regimes is \emph{caused} by in-context
system identification; the earlier interpretation does not hold up once the confound
is removed and the mechanism is probed directly.

\subsection{Does multivariate modelling help? A powered null}
\label{sec:multivariate}
The headline benchmark uses one trajectory per cell, which is adequate for the large
gaps between families but too thin to resolve small effects. To test the natural
hypothesis that a time-series model able to share information across the $2k$ state
channels should benefit from their coupling, we run a dedicated powered study:
Chronos-2 in univariate and multivariate modes, $20$ trajectories per (k, regime)
cell ($n{=}720$ paired samples), compared paired on identical trajectories.

The hypothesis does \emph{not} hold. The two modes are statistically
indistinguishable in practice: mean error $0.549$ (uni, 95\,\% CI $[0.505,0.593]$)
vs.\ $0.543$ (multi, $[0.498,0.589]$). The paired difference is negligible
($+0.005$\,rad overall) and shows \emph{no} significant effect at any $k$ or at the
discriminating horizons ($1$\,s, $10$\,s); the per-$k$ trend seen at $n{=}1$
(multivariate appearing to help most at $k{=}3$) \emph{reverses} to zero at
$n{=}720$, confirming it was sampling noise. A nominal overall $p{=}0.025$ is driven
entirely by a sub-$0.01$\,rad bias at the trivial $0.01$\,s horizon and does not
survive Holm correction. We therefore report a clean null: \textbf{cross-channel
coupling gives no practically meaningful benefit for time-series forecasting of this
coupled chaotic system.} (On the shared out-of-sample grid the Chronos family lands
at $0.68$--$0.70$\,rad---ahead of \emph{every} learned model and behind only the
numerical integrators and \texttt{kimi} in Table~\ref{tab:overall}.) This result is a
concrete illustration of why error bars matter here: an eye-catching $n{=}1$ trend
became a null under proper sampling.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{In-sample evaluation of learned dynamics is badly optimistic.} The single
most consequential result is methodological: when the learned dynamics models are
scored on the \emph{same} held-out trajectories as everyone else, rather than on
their own training data, their error roughly triples (${\sim}0.26\to0.84$--$1.06$\,rad)
and they fall \emph{below} a zero-shot time-series model and a zero-shot LLM. The
``small fitted model beats general-purpose forecasters'' conclusion that an in-sample
leaderboard invites does not survive a level playing field. For chaotic systems the
lesson is concrete: a learned simulator must be reported on trajectories it never saw,
or its headline number is largely a memorisation artefact.

\paragraph{On a level field, a frontier LLM is the strongest black-box forecaster here.}
In this benchmark, out of sample, the strongest black-box model is \texttt{kimi}
($0.538$\,rad), ahead of both
Chronos modes ($0.68$--$0.70$) and every learned dynamics model ($0.84$--$1.06$); only
the classical integrators remain clearly ahead. The training \emph{objective} still
moves the needle---rollout loss decisively beats derivative matching---but the
in-sample ``mixed-$k$ wins'' headline is a statistical null on held-out data. What
generalises is the integrator-shaped loss, not the particular window-mixing recipe.

\paragraph{Reliability is a first-class axis.} The naive answered-only leaderboard
flatters exactly the least reliable models. Scoring abstentions at the random-guess
baseline is what places \texttt{grok} (answers only $82\%$ of the held-out grid) at
rank~$8$ and the LNN ($45\%$) last. Chain-of-thought does not rescue this either:
paired CoT-vs-direct differences are statistically insignificant for every LLM (the
largest model, \texttt{kimi}, is if anything worse with CoT). Numerical integration is
inherently sequential, and a
fixed budget of reasoning tokens cannot stand in for thousands of integration steps.
Any benchmark that reports error over answered cells alone is measuring the wrong
thing for deployment.

\paragraph{No evidence of in-context system identification.} The apparent
``LLMs do better when constants are hidden'' effect---which an in-sample reading took
as a bright spot worth scaling---is a difficulty confound. In a matched experiment
that toggles \emph{only} disclosure on identical trajectories, hiding the constants is
if anything slightly \emph{worse} ($+0.069$\,rad overall; $+0.297$ at $k{=}1$), and a
direct inference probe shows the models do not recover the constants at all: they fall
back on Earth-standard priors ($g$ MAE ${\sim}1.8$--$2.1$ against true
$g\in[1.6,12]$). Under the observation and prompting conditions of this benchmark we therefore find
\emph{little evidence} that the models infer hidden physical parameters; we caution
against reading regime contrasts as inference without a confound-free matched test.

\paragraph{Cost and practicality.} Accuracy alone hides a sharp cost gap. The
out-of-sample LLM evaluation cost \$110 in API fees ($3{,}960$ cells,
${\sim}\$0.03$ per forecast) and is the \emph{only} family billed per query; the
numerical and time-series models are free at inference, and the learned models are free
after a one-time training cost (minutes to a few hours on one $8$\,GB GPU). The
strongest black-box forecaster (\texttt{kimi}) is thus also the most expensive, whereas a
model trained on the system forecasts for free at $100\%$ reliability and only modestly
higher error. We report cost beside error so the trade-off is explicit rather than
buried.

\paragraph{Scope and generality.} Our quantitative rankings are specific to this substrate
and horizon; we do not claim a particular \emph{model order} transfers unchanged to Lorenz,
driven-pendulum, or fluid systems. What \emph{does} transfer is the evaluation
\emph{protocol}---not the leaderboard it produces: in-sample scoring of a fitted simulator
is optimistic on \emph{any} system whose train and test trajectories overlap; the
reliability-adjusted metric and the matched, confound-free control for inferred-versus-disclosed
parameters are system-agnostic \emph{mechanisms}; and the cross-family, out-of-sample
comparison is a reusable design. We nonetheless expect the resulting \emph{ranking} to be
system-dependent---which models abstain or destabilise, and hence how the reliability
adjustment reorders them, may differ on another substrate---so it is the protocol, not its
outcome, that we claim transfers. The harness is substrate-parametrised (the dynamics enter only through the
ground-truth integrator and the known equations of motion), so porting it to other chaotic
systems is mechanical and is the natural next step---one we expect to change the numbers but
not the methodological conclusions.

\section{Limitations}
The out-of-sample leaderboard aggregates $180$ held-out trajectories ($60$ per regime)
with bootstrap confidence intervals, and the powered sub-studies (objective,
multivariate, system-ID) use $n{=}720$--$900$ paired samples; still, all trajectories
derive from a single held-out seed, and additional seeds would further tighten the
headline error bars. Predictions
are single-endpoint rather than full-trajectory; a full-rollout protocol would let the
predictability horizon be measured directly rather than inferred from four horizons.
The comparison remains structurally asymmetric: the learned models are trained on the
$k$-pendulum family and then tested on \emph{held-out} trajectories of it, whereas the
LLMs and Chronos are zero-shot---fair on the shared grid, but not a like-for-like
training regime. The pendulum equations are ubiquitous in training corpora, so
contamination cannot be excluded; the hidden-constants regime partially mitigates this
but does not eliminate it. Finally, we deliberately isolate the evaluation methodology on
a single, maximally-controlled substrate and three LLMs: this is what lets the
methodological effects be measured cleanly (\S\ref{sec:sysid}), but it does mean the
\emph{numerical} results are demonstrated on one system. Replicating them on other chaotic
systems (Lorenz, driven pendulum) and a broader model roster (e.g.\ Qwen-VL, TimesFM,
Moirai) through the same substrate-parametrised harness is the natural next step.

\section{Conclusion}
On a controlled $k$-pendulum testbed spanning four model families under identical
conditions and---crucially---on the \emph{same out-of-sample held-out trajectories},
the headline is methodological. In-sample evaluation of learned dynamics models is badly
optimistic: scored off their training data the learned models triple their error and
drop behind both a zero-shot time-series model and a frontier LLM, so that in this
benchmark the strongest black-box forecaster is a frontier LLM (\texttt{kimi}), while
numerical integrators remain the ceiling when the governing equations are known. The training objective, not the architecture, is what generalises---rollout
loss beats derivative matching---but the in-sample ``mixed-$k$ wins'' result is a
statistical null out of sample. Reliability-adjusted scoring and chaos-appropriate
metrics overturn the naive leaderboard, and a confound-free matched experiment plus a
direct inference probe weaken the one apparent bright spot for general models: under the observation and
prompting conditions of this benchmark, we find little evidence that the models infer
hidden physical parameters---instead they default to Earth-standard priors. The lesson worth carrying forward is
methodological---test learned simulators out of sample, score abstentions, and demand a
matched control before crediting inference.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Code, exact prompts (Appendix~A), trained weights, the
shared held-out specification, and per-cell checkpoints are released with the benchmark.
Training trajectories use seed $42$; the held-out evaluation set uses a \emph{disjoint}
seed ($\texttt{20260620}$), $180$ trajectories ($60$ per regime). LLMs
(\texttt{kimi-k2.6}, \texttt{grok-4-1-fast-reasoning}, \texttt{deepseek-v4-pro}, via
Azure AI Foundry, evaluated June~2026) are sampled \emph{once} at temperature $0$ (no
best-of-$N$). A cell is scored as \emph{answered} iff it returns a parseable, finite
prediction; otherwise it is assigned the reliability-adjusted baseline $\pi/2$. All local
training and evaluation run on a single consumer GPU (8\,GB); no proprietary training
data is used.

You are a careful physicist predicting the future state of a k-pendulum (a chain of

step-by-step about the dynamics, then output your final answer. Wrap the final answer
in \texttt{<answer>...</answer>} tags containing ONLY a single JSON object:
\{"theta": [..k floats..], "omega": [..k floats..]\}.''

\paragraph{User prompt (coordinate modality).}
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
Number of links k = {k}
Physical constants:
  g (gravity, m/s^2): {g}
  L (rod lengths, m): {L}
  m (point masses, kg): {m}
  damping (viscous coefficient on omega): {damping}
Initial state at t=0:
  theta_0 (rad): {theta0}
  omega_0 (rad/s): {omega0}
Predict the state at t = {T} seconds.
Return theta and omega as length-{k} JSON arrays.
\end{verbatim}\end{small}

\paragraph{Hidden-constants regime.} The constants block above is replaced by:
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
Physical constants: HIDDEN. The numerical values of gravity, rod lengths, point

conditions alone.
\end{verbatim}\end{small}

\paragraph{Parsing and scoring.} Each response is stripped of code fences and (for
CoT) of everything outside the \texttt{<answer>...</answer>} tags; the first
\texttt{\{...\}} object is JSON-parsed and validated to contain length-$k$
\texttt{theta} and \texttt{omega} arrays. Any response failing these checks is counted
as \emph{unanswered} and scored at the reliability-adjusted baseline $\pi/2$.
