\begin{abstract}
Learned forecasters increasingly stand in for scientific models that are themselves imperfect,
and a forecast score alone cannot say whether the surrogate succeeds for the right reasons. We
test five conditions that connect a score to a scientific claim (held-out generalization,
complete failure accounting, matched disclosure, structural identifiability, and cross-system
transfer) on $k$-pendulum ($k\in\{1,2,3\}$) and Lorenz--63 forecasts from
language models, a time-series foundation model, learned dynamics, sparse identification, and
classical integrators. Every test overturns a conclusion the raw comparison invites: the
in-sample/out-of-sample gap is fourfold, two exact symmetries make the apparent parameter
recovery impossible, and a second system reverses the model ordering.
A correct leaderboard can still support incorrect scientific conclusions. We release the
prompts, outputs, checkpoints, seeds, and compute records needed to repeat the audit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Scientific claims require more than low forecast error. When a learned forecaster stands in
for an imperfect scientific model, a result may depend on training trajectories, dropped
failures, unmatched inputs, an unidentifiable target, or one favorable system: failures of the
evaluation rather than of the surrogate. We study these failure modes directly. The task is
to predict a chaotic system's state $\mathbf{s}_T$ from its state $\mathbf{s}_0$, on two
systems chosen to disagree: the $k$-link pendulum, chaotic for $k{\ge}2$, with high-precision
ground truth and control over what physical metadata a model is told, and Lorenz--63, which
differs in observation model and hypothesis class.

Our contribution is a claim audit for dynamical-system forecasts: held-out generalization,
complete failure accounting, matched disclosure on identical trajectories, identifiability
checked before scoring recovery, varied training budgets, and transfer to a second system.
Table~\ref{tab:audit-summary} states the conclusion before and after each test; all five
alter or reject a conclusion the unaudited comparison supports. These are conclusions the
field actually draws: zero-shot forecasters are routinely scored without failure accounting
or matched controls \citep{gruver2023llmtime,zhang2025zeroshotchaos}, and our setting mirrors
that practice. Three of the checks are the familiar concerns
of imperfect models applied to the evaluation itself: misspecification (a Hamiltonian prior
on damped dynamics), identifiability (exact parameter degeneracies), and transfer (a
hypothesis class containing one system and not the other).

\section{Benchmark Design}
\subsection{Task and systems}
The pendulum state is $\mathbf{s}_0=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k,\omega_1,\dots,\omega_k)\in
\mathbb{R}^{2k}$; hiding its constants does not change the trajectory, which permits the
matched test in \S\ref{sec:sysid}. Ground-truth trajectories integrate the full nonlinear
Lagrangian equations of motion with fixed-step RK4 at $\Delta t{=}10^{-3}$\,s, recording
states every $0.01$\,s plus a $10$\,s pre-$t{=}0$ context window (used only by the
time-series models). Initial conditions are sampled $\theta_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-3,3)$\,rad,
$\omega_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)$\,rad/s with a fixed seed; because the labels come from RK4,
the coarser-step RK4 baseline is a reference by construction, not a competitor.

\subsection{Regimes and grid}
Three regimes test generalization to non-standard constants (Table~\ref{tab:regimes}); in
\texttt{changed\_hidden} the constants $(g,L,m,\text{damping})$ are not disclosed to the
model. An evaluation cell is a tuple (model, $k$, regime, horizon), with horizons
$\{0.01,1,10,60\}$\,s. The full LLM grid also varies input modality and prompting; every
comparison here uses the coordinate, direct setting, and the other axes appear only in the
released records.

\paragraph{Out-of-sample protocol.} Learned models are evaluated off their training data: a
shared held-out set (a separate seed) of $180$ initial conditions ($60$ per regime,
$k\in\{1,2,3\}$) is scored identically for every family. The cross-family comparison uses
horizons $\{1,10\}$\,s, where chaos has begun but error has not saturated
($180\times2=360$ cells per model); in-sample error appears only once, to quantify the size
of the generalization gap (\S\ref{sec:reliability}).

\subsection{Inputs and grouping}
\label{sec:inputs}
Each family sees different information, and we treat that as part of the experimental record
(Table~\ref{tab:inputs}, Appendix~B). The integrators get the exact equations and constants;
the learned models get target-system training plus the constants (normal-regime priors in
hidden cells); the LLMs get a text description with the constants except in hidden cells;
Chronos gets a $10$\,s pre-$t{=}0$ history window and no constants at all. A further
asymmetry runs the other way: the LLMs are large proprietary models while the learned models
are small MLPs (Appendix~B), so larger physics-informed networks are outside our scope. We
therefore separate known-physics methods (RK4, Euler, symplectic), which serve as numerical
references, from the black-box methods being compared.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
The primary error is the mean absolute angle error (radians), wrapped to $[-\pi,\pi]$ and
averaged over links and trajectories.

\paragraph{Reliability-adjusted error.} A cell is answered only if the model returns a
parseable, finite prediction. Because scoring answered cells alone would reward abstention on
hard cells, unanswered cells receive the random-guess baseline $\pi/2$ (the expected
wrapped-angle error of a uniform guess); answering is part of the score.

\paragraph{Verification record.} Each cell retains its identifiers, prompt, raw response,
parser outcome, prediction, token counts, and score. Local methods are reproducible from the
released checkpoints and seeds; hosted deployments can change or be withdrawn, so for them the
released per-cell responses are the record, keeping the API evaluation auditable after the
deployments it measured are gone.

\paragraph{Disclosure gap.} We define
$\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}=\mathrm{err}(\text{hidden})-\mathrm{err}(\text{disclosed})$; positive
values indicate a penalty for withholding constants. Because regime contrasts confound
disclosure with trajectory difficulty, \S\ref{sec:sysid} also toggles disclosure on identical
trajectories.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\paragraph{LLMs.} We evaluate \texttt{kimi-k2.6}, \texttt{grok-4-1-fast-reasoning}, and
\texttt{deepseek-v4-pro} through an OpenAI-compatible API (Azure AI Foundry deployments;
evaluated June~2026), with a structured prompt and JSON answer schema (Appendix~A); each cell
is one temperature-$0$ call with uniform token and timeout budgets, no best-of-$N$.

\paragraph{Time-series foundation models.} We run Chronos~\citep{ansari2024chronos} locally
in its standard history-conditioned form: the $10$\,s pre-$t{=}0$ context window of all $2k$
state channels forecasts to the target horizon. The comparison uses \texttt{chronos-2} in
univariate and multivariate modes; the paired mode comparison uses $20$ trajectories per
(k, regime) cell.

\paragraph{Learned dynamics.} Neural~ODE, HNN, and LNN share a $256$-wide, $3$-layer tanh
MLP and integrate at inference with \texttt{scipy}, comparing derivative matching, rollout,
and mixed-$k$ rollout objectives (Appendix~B). The primary models train on nine trajectories,
one per (k, regime) cell; a nested budget study trains fresh checkpoints at $\{1,5,20\}$
trajectories per eligible cell with three initializations (HNN: undamped cells only, since
damping violates Hamiltonian conservation).

\paragraph{Integrators.} Euler, RK4, and St\"ormer--Verlet (symplectic) at $\Delta
t{=}0.01$\,s using the exact equations of motion; their error is from discretization alone.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Four reference baselines use the same grid: persistence
($\theta_T{=}\theta_0$), constant velocity ($\theta_T{=}\theta_0{+}\omega_0T$), a
small-angle linearized model, and a nearest-neighbor analog forecast built from the same data
as the learned models. On the balanced two-seed subset we also fit SINDy (degree-two library,
STLSQ) and NVAR (two delays, degree-two features, ridge); divergent rollouts are retained as
failures at $\pi/2$.

\paragraph{Lorenz replication.} A secondary Lorenz--63 test compares numerical references,
persistence, SINDy, NVAR, a Neural~ODE trained on 20 seed-42 trajectories, and zero-shot
Chronos-2 on five five-trajectory held-out seeds, reporting training-normalized coordinate MAE
at $\{0.1,0.5,1.0\}$~s (configuration in Appendix~C). HNN and LNN are omitted because
Lorenz--63 is dissipative and non-Hamiltonian.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Counting failed responses}
\label{sec:reliability}
Every model is evaluated on the shared held-out grid of $360$ cells (180 trajectories,
horizons $\{1,10\}$\,s); the Answered column in Table~\ref{tab:overall} gives the fraction
with valid predictions. \texttt{grok} answers $82\%$ and \texttt{lnn} $94\%$: their ranks
fall when missing cells receive $\pi/2$; a mean over answered cells alone would flatter both.
Intervals are $95\%$ bootstrap over trajectories; LLM intervals exclude per-call sampling
variation.

Table~\ref{tab:overall} ranks the models under the access conditions of \S\ref{sec:inputs};
the ranking is conditional on those inputs, this training budget, and this system
(\S\ref{sec:lorenz} tests transfer). The known-equation integrators sit at the top as
references, and every learned dynamics model lands closer to the $\pi/2$ baseline than to
the integrators. The lowest-error black-box entry is the LLM \texttt{kimi} ($0.538$\,rad,
$99\%$ answered), and its marginal interval excludes the nearest baseline's. That is a
statement about one recorded June~2026 deployment, not about model families; the Lorenz test
rejects the family reading.

After equalizing the hidden-regime inputs, nearest-neighbor lookup ($0.725$) and the
linearized pendulum ($0.855$) beat every learned model, and persistence ($1.004$) beats all

score about $0.26$\,rad on their training trajectories and $0.97$--$1.14$\,rad held-out, so
in-sample evaluation understates error about fourfold.

SINDy ($0.883$\,rad) and NVAR ($1.248$) appear only on the balanced two-seed subset
(Table~\ref{tab:replication}); SINDy lands between Chronos and the learned models, NVAR near
constant velocity.

\subsection{Matched disclosure test}
\label{sec:sysid}
On the shared held-out set the hidden regime shows lower error than the disclosed
($-0.390$\,rad, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{<}10^{-3}$, unpaired) and normal ($-0.524$) regimes. This
looks favorable to withholding constants, but the regimes use different trajectories, and the
hidden ones are slower and less divergent. When we instead toggle disclosure on identical
trajectories (Table~\ref{tab:sysid}), the pooled effect reverses: hiding the constants
increases error ($\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}{=}{+}0.069$\,rad, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.034$), with
disclosure helping strongly at $k{=}1$ and only a small reverse effect at $k{=}3$. The
unadjusted hidden-regime advantage is thus a difficulty confound.

The disclosure test also has a structural limit. The angular equations are unchanged by
$(m,d)\mapsto(am,ad)$ and by $(L,g,d)\mapsto(bL,bg,b^2d)$, so for $k{=}1$ only $g/L$ and
$d/(mL^2)$ are identifiable from an angle trajectory; absolute $g$, $L$, $m$, and $d$ cannot
be scored as independently recoverable. Asked to name the constants from a single state, the
LLMs mostly returned Earth-standard defaults, which is evidence of a prior, not of recovery;
a released numerical check confirms both invariances hold to below $10^{-13}$.

\subsection{Replication across seeds and training budgets}
\label{sec:replication}
We reran the local pendulum subset on an independent held-out seed (five trajectories per
cell versus 20; Table~\ref{tab:replication}). The selected ordering is unchanged: numerical
references lowest, Chronos-2 before the learned models, HNN-rollout best among them. Two seeds
do not estimate seed-level uncertainty. The LNN's low primary-seed coverage was a hardware
artifact: its per-step Hessian exhausted the GPU on long rollouts. A CPU rerun recovers the
dropped cells ($66\%\to94\%$; both seeds use it); the remaining failures are genuine
divergences, scored at $\pi/2$, and the two-seed mean stays $1.366$\,rad.

\paragraph{Training-budget check.}
Across three initializations and both held-out seeds, Neural~ODE error falls from $1.090$ at
one trajectory per cell to $0.861$ at twenty, and normal-regime HNN error from $1.269$ to
$0.981$ (Table~\ref{tab:budget}); the rows cover different regimes and are not an architecture
comparison. More data helps the Neural~ODE monotonically, the HNN gains plateau by $15$
trajectories, and neither closes the gap to known physics.

\subsection{Cross-system test: Lorenz--63}
\label{sec:lorenz}
We test whether the pendulum conclusions transfer to Lorenz--63, a chaotic system with a
polynomial vector field. They do not. The identifiability verdict flips: on the pendulum the
physical constants are not recoverable from an angle trajectory (\S\ref{sec:sysid}), while on
Lorenz, SINDy recovers the governing equations themselves, not merely their forecasts. The
refit selects exactly the true sparsity pattern and returns
$(\hat\sigma,\hat\rho,\hat\beta)=(10,28,8/3)$ to coefficient error $2\times10^{-13}$
(Appendix~C); forecast error stays below $2\times10^{-5}$ in every seed. The difference is
the setting, not the audit: Lorenz--63 lies inside SINDy's hypothesis class and is fully
observed; the pendulum's absolute constants are unidentifiable from angles under any method.
Identifiability is a property of the observation model and the hypothesis class, and the
check makes it explicit before recovery is scored.

The model ordering also changes. Trained on one seed and evaluated on five held-out seeds
(25 trajectories), a Lorenz Neural~ODE reaches normalized MAE $0.014\pm0.008$, $0.079\pm0.030$,
and $0.277\pm0.063$ at $0.1$, $0.5$, and $1.0$~s (mean$\,\pm\,$SE over seeds), below both
Chronos-2 modes at every horizon (multivariate $0.101\pm0.018$,
$0.691\pm0.129$, $0.771\pm0.097$; univariate similar); NVAR drifts to $0.429$ by $1.0$~s and
persistence trails ($0.900$; Table~\ref{tab:replication}). This reverses the pendulum, where trained models lost to
trivial baselines: here the system-trained model beats the zero-shot foundation model. The
families again see different inputs, so this is not an architecture race; what the
cross-system result rejects is the pendulum ranking as a general ordering of model families.

The reversal does not rest on a favorable seed: the Neural~ODE beats both Chronos-2 modes in
every one of the $15$ seed-by-horizon comparisons, and the identifiability half is not a
sampling claim at all, since the pendulum symmetries are exact and Lorenz--63 lies in SINDy's
library by construction. The test excludes the API LLMs, HNN, and LNN (a Hamiltonian
surrogate is misspecified for the dissipative Lorenz flow). Those omissions limit what it can
establish, not what it can refute: one system on which both verdicts invert is enough to
withdraw the generalization, and the Lorenz ordering is not offered as the correct one.

\subsection{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:behavior}
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} reports five further checks; two matter most here. First, the
physical-fidelity check localizes a misspecification: on the undamped regime the lowest
learned-model energy drift ($0.78$, $E^{*}$-normalized) far exceeds the symplectic
integrator's ($0.066$), and a symplectic HNN rollout leaves drift nearly unchanged, so the
fault is the learned Hamiltonian, not the integrator. Second,
every black-box family saturates near the chaotic noise floor by $1$\,s, hence the
$\{1,10\}$\,s comparison window. The rest: error rises monotonically with $k$; the hidden
regime is easier for most models (the \S\ref{sec:sysid} confound); rollout loss is not
uniformly better than derivative matching; and Chronos-2's two modes are indistinguishable on
$720$ paired samples.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
Performance depends on the system, observations, hypothesis class, data, and access, and a
scalar leaderboard does not encode those conditions: each of the five checks changes a claim
the raw leaderboard supports. Forecasting benchmarks should report the checks, training
budgets, and information access as part of the result. We chose systems with cheap ground truth so each check could be shown to
change a conclusion; the checks matter most where real observations meet an imperfect
simulator, verification is expensive, and misspecification cannot be ruled out by
construction. All prompts, outputs, checkpoints, seeds, and compute records accompany the
paper.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\label{sec:limitations}
The LLM grid uses one held-out seed, the local replication a smaller second, the budget study
three initializations, and the Lorenz test five trajectories per seed without the API LLMs,
HNN, or LNN. SINDy's recovery requires the library to contain the true degree, predictions
are endpoint states, and pendulum-like data may appear in the foundation models' corpora.

\section{Prompt templates}
The prompts used for every LLM evaluation cell are reproduced below verbatim.

\paragraph{System prompt (direct / no-CoT).}
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
You are a careful physicist predicting the future state of a k-pendulum (a chain of
k point masses on massless rods, hinged in a planar chain, swinging under gravity
with viscous damping). Respond with ONLY a single JSON object on one line, no prose,
no code fences. Schema:
{"theta": [..k floats..], "omega": [..k floats..]}
theta_i is in radians (link i measured from straight-down,
positive counter-clockwise)
and omega_i is angular velocity in rad/s.
\end{verbatim}\end{small}

\paragraph{System prompt (chain-of-thought).} Same preamble, then: ``Reason step-by-step
about the dynamics, then output your final answer. Wrap the final answer in
\texttt{<answer>...</answer>} tags containing ONLY a single JSON object:
\{"theta": [..k floats..], "omega": [..k floats..]\}.''

\paragraph{User prompt (coordinate modality).}
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
Number of links k = {k}
Physical constants:
  g (gravity, m/s^2): {g}
  L (rod lengths, m): {L}
  m (point masses, kg): {m}
  damping (viscous coefficient on omega): {damping}
Initial state at t=0:
  theta_0 (rad): {theta0}
  omega_0 (rad/s): {omega0}
Predict the state at t = {T} seconds.
Return theta and omega as length-{k} JSON arrays.
\end{verbatim}\end{small}

\paragraph{Hidden-constants regime.} The constants block above is replaced by:
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
Physical constants: HIDDEN. The numerical values of gravity, rod lengths, point
masses, and damping are NOT disclosed. You must infer dynamics from the initial
conditions alone.
\end{verbatim}\end{small}

\paragraph{Parsing and scoring.} Each response is stripped of code fences and (for CoT) of
everything outside the \texttt{<answer>...</answer>} tags; the first \texttt{\{...\}} object
is JSON-parsed and validated to contain length-$k$ \texttt{theta} and \texttt{omega} arrays.
A response that fails any check is counted as unanswered and scored at $\pi/2$.

All primary learned models use a $256$-wide, $3$-layer tanh MLP, Adam, and a cosine-annealed
learning rate, trained on a single 8\,GB consumer GPU. Mixed-$k$ rollout adds negligible
parameters; its cost is wall-clock (the HNN mixed-$k$ run is the most expensive at
$\sim$6.7\,h because each unroll step recomputes $\nabla H$ via autograd). Chronos runs at
inference only. The
dataset has $67{,}491$ samples across $k\in\{1,2,3\}$ and three regimes, subsampled at
$\Delta t{=}0.01$\,s from the $10^{-3}$\,s ground truth. Despite the number of samples, the
training set contains only \emph{nine} trajectories, one for each (k, regime) cell. The
budget study uses nested subsets of 1, 5, and 20 trajectories per eligible cell and three
training initializations. Neural~ODE therefore sees 9, 45, or 180 trajectories; HNN sees 3,
15, or 60 undamped trajectories. Evaluation uses the same two held-out seeds throughout.

\paragraph{Training objectives.} Derivative matching minimizes MSE on finite-difference
$\dot{\mathbf{s}}$; rollout loss minimizes MSE on the state after $k$ Euler steps with
gradients through the unroll; mixed-$k$ rollout trains on several window lengths jointly
(Neural~ODE: $k\in\{10,50\}$; HNN: $k\in\{5,20\}$). The LNN's loss requires second-order
autodifferentiation and is trained at full scale ($67$k samples, hidden 256) on a CUDA GPU.
Chronos-2 resamples the context to up to $512$ points and forecasts $\le 64$ steps.

\paragraph{Reproducibility records.} The anonymized artifact accompanying this submission
contains the code, prompts, trained weights, held-out specifications, and the per-cell
records for the main held-out comparison ($2{,}160$ records), the matched disclosure test
($1{,}800$), the modality and prompting grid, and the Lorenz sweep; the two large sweeps, the
training-budget and balanced-seed studies, are packaged as separate archives. All dataset and
held-out seeds are fixed and recorded in the released configuration. LLMs are sampled once at
temperature 0. The reported hosted-model numbers recompute from the saved records; re-calling
the API is a new evaluation rather than a reproduction. Invalid cells score $\pi/2$.

\section{Seed and system replication}

\section{Related work}
\label{app:related}
The audited models span learned dynamics \citep{chen2018node,greydanus2019hnn,cranmer2020lnn}
on chaotic testbeds \citep{gilpin2021chaos}, zero-shot LLM and foundation-model forecasting
\citep{gruver2023llmtime,zhang2025zeroshotchaos,ansari2024chronos,das2024timesfm,woo2024moirai},
LLM physical reasoning \citep{lewkowycz2022minerva,wei2022cot,xu2025ugphysics}, and sparse
identification \citep{brunton2016sindy,gauthier2021nvar}. The identifiability check draws on
structural identifiability under parameter symmetries \citep{borgqvist2024symmetries}. Unlike
selective prediction, which reports risk together with coverage \citep{geifman2017selective},
failed cells here stay in the main score because models are compared at fixed task coverage.

\section{Extended diagnostics}
\label{app:diagnostics}
This appendix expands the diagnostics summarized in \S\ref{sec:behavior}.

\paragraph{Behavior by horizon.}
Out-of-sample error by horizon appears in Table~\ref{tab:horizon}. At $1$\,s the ordering
follows the main comparison: the integrators are near-exact, \texttt{kimi} ($0.457$) has the
lowest black-box error, and the learned models are at $0.86$--$1.25$. At $10$\,s all models
are in the chaotic regime, and the ordering is stable: \texttt{kimi} ($0.619$) remains lowest
among black-box methods while the learned models reach $1.09$--$1.46$. The mixed-$k$
Neural~ODE is mid-pack at both horizons ($0.951$ / $1.120$).
Figure~\ref{fig:errorgrowth} shows the growth curves over the
$\{0.01,1,10,60\}$\,s ladder: the numerical integrators stay near zero until ${\sim}10$\,s,
whereas every black-box family saturates near the chaotic noise floor by $1$\,s. The
$\{1,10\}$\,s window used for the comparison is the range over which the families remain
separable.

\paragraph{Breakdown by $k$ and regime.}
Tables~\ref{tab:perk} and~\ref{tab:perregime} report out-of-sample error by
number of links and by regime. Error increases monotonically with $k$ for every family (for
\texttt{kimi}, $0.11\to0.55\to0.95$; the integrators are near-exact at $k{=}1$ and degrade as
chaos sets in), and the cross-family ordering holds within each $k$. The
\texttt{changed\_hidden} regime has the lowest error for almost every model (for
\texttt{kimi}, $0.46$ hidden vs.\ $0.64$ normal). For the learned models, withholding the
hidden constants reduces but does not remove the same difficulty effect: hidden-regime errors
are now $0.71$--$1.19$; the previous values favored models given the true constants. The
low-gravity hidden constants ($g{=}1.62$) produce slower, less-divergent motion over a fixed
horizon, so the unadjusted lower hidden-regime error contrast is a difficulty confound
(\S\ref{sec:sysid}). A cluster bootstrap over complete held-out trajectories changes no
confidence interval by more than ${\sim}0.03$\,rad and leaves the ranking unchanged.

\paragraph{Training-objective check.}
Table~\ref{tab:objective} reports the effect of the loss with architecture fixed. Under the
equalized hidden-constant analysis, rollout loss is not uniformly better: mixed-$k$ rollout
is lower for the Neural~ODE, while single-$k$ rollout is lower for the HNN. Full-scale CUDA
training of the LNN gives the highest learned-model error ($1.356$), with $6\%$ of cells
diverging or exceeding the rollout timeout after memory-safe recovery. We make no
architecture-wide claim about LNNs; under our implementation and protocol the LNN was
numerically unstable and generalized poorly out of sample.

\paragraph{Physical-fidelity check.}
Hamiltonian and Lagrangian networks target structure preservation, so we evaluate energy
drift on the undamped (\texttt{normal}) regime, where true energy is constant. Table~\ref{tab:energy}
reports mean absolute drift over $10$\,s, normalized by $E^{*}{=}g\sum_i m_i L_i$, and the
fraction of bounded rollouts. The learned models do not match classical integrators on this
axis: the lowest-drift learned model is $0.78$, compared with $0.066$ for the symplectic
integrator and $0.494$ for Euler. Symplectic rollout of the HNN leaves drift nearly unchanged,
so changing the integrator does not account for the HNN's drift. At the derivative-matching
objective, HNN drift is comparable to Neural~ODE drift ($4.27$ vs $4.35$); the LNN is
unstable, with $27\%$ bounded rollouts.
These results use the same nine-trajectory learned-model budget as the main comparison.

\paragraph{Multivariate time-series check.}
On $720$ paired samples, Chronos-2 multivariate and univariate errors are indistinguishable:
$0.543$ (95\% CI $[0.498,0.589]$) versus $0.549$ ($[0.505,0.593]$). No effect remains after
Holm correction, so the larger study rejects the apparent per-$k$ trend in the primary grid.

\paragraph{Predictability horizon.}
Pointwise error past the Lyapunov time saturates to a system-dependent constant for every
model, so long-horizon error rankings carry little information. We define the predictability
horizon as the longest horizon a model tracks contiguously below a threshold $\tau$ from
$t{=}0$; the contiguous rule is used because, after divergence, a later horizon can fall below
$\tau$ by angle-wrap coincidence. Table~\ref{tab:predhorizon} sweeps $\tau$.
